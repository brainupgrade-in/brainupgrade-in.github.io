Prioritising Technical Debt With Evidence, Not Vibes

How I stopped guessing and started using observability data to fix what actually breaks production.

  • You cannot fix what you cannot measure; silence is not stability.
  • Agentic AI tools amplify the cost of bad architecture.
  • Evidence-based triage reduces refactor time by 40-50% compared to gut-feel approaches.

The Myth of the "Clean Code" Fantasy

I spent the first decade of my career in the trenches at JPMorgan Chase and Deutsche Bank, watching brilliant engineers burn out trying to achieve a mythical state of "clean code." We treated technical debt as a moral failing. If your code wasn’t perfectly modular, testable, and documented, you were "bad." It was toxic, it was wrong, and it was ineffective.

In 2026, the landscape has shifted. We are no longer just managing monolithic Java services; we are orchestrating complex agentic workflows, micro-frontends, and distributed event streams. The cost of technical debt has escalated from a minor annoyance to a critical business risk. But here is the hard truth: most engineering leaders are still prioritizing debt based on vibes, not evidence.

I see teams spending weeks refactoring a legacy authentication service because the lead architect "felt" it was ugly, while the payment gateway—where 90% of our latency spikes occur—remains untouched because its code is "simple." This is not engineering. This is vanity.

We need to shift from aesthetic judgment to empirical triage. Technical debt prioritization evidence 2026 is not about finding the ugliest function. It is about finding the most expensive one.

Stop Guessing, Start Measuring: The Observability Imperative

You cannot prioritize what you cannot measure. If you are not tracking the cost of debt in your production environment, you are flying blind. The first step in evidence-based prioritization is establishing a baseline of operational pain.

I use a simple framework to quantify debt, focusing on three core metrics:

  • Change Failure Rate (CFR): How often do deployments fail? High CFR in a specific service indicates brittle code or poor test coverage—classic debt.
  • Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR): When things break, how long does it take to fix? High MTTR suggests complex dependencies and lack of observability.
  • Code Churn vs. Bug Density: Areas of code that are frequently modified but also generate frequent incidents are debt hotspots.

At Morgan Stanley, we stopped asking "Is this code clean?" and started asking "What does this code cost us when it breaks?" By correlating incident reports with Git blame data, we identified that 20% of our services were responsible for 80% of our post-incident review time. That is your target. Ignore the rest for now.

The Agentic AI Multiplier Effect

Why is this urgent now? Because of Agentic AI. As we integrate autonomous agents into our development and operations pipelines, the cost of bad architecture increases exponentially. An AI agent working in a well-structured, documented, and observable system is a force multiplier. An agent working in a legacy spaghetti codebase with no context is a liability.

If your agents cannot reliably navigate your service mesh, they will hallucinate in production. I wrote about this in detail in my post on Agentic AI Control Plane for Enterprise 2026. The control plane is only as good as the underlying infrastructure. If you have high technical debt, your AI agents will inherit that chaos.

Consider agent tool calling reliability. If your APIs are inconsistent, poorly documented, or unstable, your agents will fail. I discussed the failure modes of agent tool calling in Agent Tool Calling Reliability and Failure Modes. A high failure rate in tool calling is not just a bug; it is evidence of architectural debt. Prioritize stabilizing these interfaces before you add more intelligence on top.

Triaging Debt: The Business Impact Matrix

Once you have your data, you need a framework to prioritize. I use a 2x2 matrix based on Business Impact and Implementation Effort. But unlike the standard agile matrix, I weight Business Impact heavily because time is money.

  1. High Impact, High Effort: These are your "Big Rocks." Refactor the payment gateway, the core ledger, or the customer-facing API. Schedule these for dedicated quarters, not sprints.
  2. High Impact, Low Effort: These are your "Quick Wins." Fix the logging in the auth service, add missing metrics to the cache layer. Do these immediately.
  3. Low Impact, High Effort: These are "Time Sinks." Avoid these unless you are decommissioning the service. Do not refactor a legacy reporting tool that no one uses.
  4. Low Impact, Low Effort: These are "Nice to Haves." Handle these during routine maintenance or when context-switching.

The key is to be ruthless. If a service has low business value and high debt, the evidence says: decommission it. Do not refactor it. At ExlService, we decommissioned three legacy reporting services that had high churn but low usage, saving $200k annually in compute and maintenance costs. That is evidence-based debt management.

Measuring Success: Beyond the Code

How do you know your prioritization is working? You track the reduction in operational burden. I look for a 40-50% reduction in time spent on firefighting after a major refactoring initiative. This is not a soft metric; it is a hard business outcome.

Also, monitor the stability of your AI agents. If your agents are completing tasks with higher success rates and lower latency, your architectural improvements are paying off. I covered agent observability in Agent Observability: LangSmith, Competitors, Arize, Phoenix, Weights & Biases. Use these tools to prove that your debt reduction efforts are improving agent reliability.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How do I justify technical debt refactoring to non-technical stakeholders?

Speak their language: risk and revenue. Do not talk about code quality. Talk about deployment frequency, incident rates, and customer satisfaction. Show them that high debt leads to slower feature delivery and higher risk of outages. Use data from your observability platform to quantify the cost of inaction.

2. What if my team is too busy to refactor?

Then you are already paying the interest. You cannot have it both ways. Allocate 20% of your sprint capacity to debt reduction, or accept that your velocity will continue to decline. If you are too busy to refactor, you are too busy to succeed. Prioritize the highest-impact items from your matrix.

3. Can I use AI to identify technical debt?

Yes, but with caution. AI can analyze code patterns and correlate them with incident data, but it cannot understand business context. Use AI as a suggestion engine, not a decision maker. Validate AI recommendations against your production metrics. I discussed AI agent memory and context in Agent Memory Architecture for Long-Term Context. Ensure your AI tools have accurate context to avoid false positives in debt identification.

4. How often should I re-evaluate my technical debt priorities?

At least quarterly, or after any major architectural change. Business priorities shift, and so do the costs of debt. Regularly review your 2x2 matrix and adjust based on new evidence from production.
